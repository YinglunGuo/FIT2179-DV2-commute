# Moodle description (under 500 words)

**Domain, why and who.** This visualisation explores how Australians — Victorians in particular — travelled to work on Census day 2021, and how the pandemic Census broke 50 years of slow-moving commuting patterns. The topic matters because commuting decisions shape household time and cost, congestion, access to jobs, infrastructure spending and emissions. The intended audience is the general Australian public — especially Victorian residents and university students — who understand the everyday experience of commuting but who have not seen the Census data side-by-side.

**What.** All figures come from public Australian Bureau of Statistics sources: the *Australia's Journey to Work* article (2022) for national, state and PT-mode tables; the 2021 ABS QuickStats and Community Profiles for state, capital-city and LGA-level data; the ABS state-by-state WFH table 1976–2021 for the long-run view; and the ABS digital boundary files (ASGS Edition 3) for the simplified Victoria outline. Eighteen Victorian LGAs are used, spanning Inner city, Inner / Middle / Outer suburb, Peri-urban and Regional zones. LGA-level mode shares use the id.com.au community-profile "private vehicle" composite, which is ABS-derived; state and national figures use the ABS strict "Car as driver" definition. All source URLs are linked from each chart's source-note and the README.

**How (Munzner's What / Why / How framework).** *What*: a table of employed persons by main travel-to-work mode, broken by year, state, capital-city vs rest-of-state, LGA and PT mode — categorical, ordinal and quantitative attributes. *Why*: compare modes within an area, distribute area differences across the state, identify trends across Census years, find correlations, look up specific LGAs. *How* the encoding maps: a hero donut for composition (01); heatmap and grouped bars for area × mode (02, 03); a three-panel geographic view combining a Victoria-wide bubble map, a Greater Melbourne inset and a car-vs-PT scatter sharing one zone palette (04); a wide ranked bar with state-average rule (05); a second map idiom — equal-area grid cartogram of all 19 LGAs (15) — because dense inner-Melbourne LGAs are hidden by overlap on the bubble map; a custom small-multiples panel of all eight states' WFH trajectories 1976–2021 (06); time-series lines for change (07, 09, 11); a sorted dumbbell for city-vs-rest gaps (10); a normalised stacked bar for vehicle ownership (13); a stacked area for the 45-year active-transport story (14); a ranked bar of the 2021 mode hierarchy (12).

Marks and channels follow Munzner's recommendations: position-on-common-scale is used wherever ordering matters; sequential blue encodes the heatmap's single quantitative variable; categorical colour stays consistent across the project (red = car, blue = PT, green = WFH, orange/purple = active). Every chart has on-figure annotations marking key inflections, references and outliers; tooltips reveal the raw value. The structure is a scrollable four-chapter story, not a dashboard, because the audience is non-expert and the analytical message is a sequence, not a query.
